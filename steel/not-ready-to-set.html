Not Ready to Set

Every outfit on the job counts down to “before you set,” and you’re the one who finds out at six in the morning — with a crane, an operator and a raising gang already on the clock — which of them didn’t make it. Walk the pick before you call the iron up and name what stops the set, in your words: the anchor bolts that aren’t in or nobody’s shot, the embeds that never got cast, the deck below that isn’t poured, the crane that can’t get set up, the laydown that’s still full, the line over the swing nobody de-energized. Then send the only ask that matters, and get the answer in writing — fix it and tell me who and when, or give me a new day for the crew and the crane. There’s no answer that flies it as it sits: a hot line, a bolt, an embed, the deck below and a detail that doesn’t match clear with whoever owns them, never on somebody else’s say-so. Because a crane day doesn’t come back, and iron you set on somebody else’s open condition is on your record.

What you send

It’s a condition list, not a verdict. Nothing on it says anybody’s anchor bolts, embeds or deck failed anything — it says what you found, where, in your words, with anything you checked taken off your own level and set beside what the approved erection set says. It states no torque, no bolt tension, no weld size, no capacity, no survey elevation and no threshold of any kind, and it never says a connection is ready to load or a bolt is right — that is the engineer’s call and the special inspector’s numbered record. It doesn’t stop the job; it holds this pick and hands whoever runs the job the choice, on the record — fix it with who and when, or move the crew and the crane to a new day. No reply to it is permission to fly the iron as it sits or to change engineered steel.

✦ A hold your crane mornings hit that isn’t on the list? Wish for it — it gets added, and you get the credit.
