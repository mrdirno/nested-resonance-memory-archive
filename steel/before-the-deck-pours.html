Before the Deck Pours

Five kits on this rack route a list at you — embeds and weld clips, loose lintels and angle, a roof opening, a dunnage stand, steel for a hanger, anchor bolts and templates. Every one of them lands in your iron or your deck, and the deck pours once. After it’s screwed off and the pour goes green, a clip nobody welded is a field weld an inspector has to sign, and an opening nobody framed is a core bit through cured concrete — and it reads on the job like your steel held them up. So walk the deck before it closes and put a row on every leave-out somebody else needs in it: whose it is, what it is, which column line, whether it’s on the drawing, set, set and tied, not coming, or already covered, and the gate it has to beat. Send it to each outfit while it’s still a clip.

What you were told and what you saw, not a certification. Every row is a leave-out somebody else needs in your steel, in your words, off your own walk and the approved set named at the top. This page carries no torque, no weld size or map, no bolt tension, no material grade, no capacity and no embed tolerance — the shop drawing and the engineer own all of that, and it cites the sheet, never restates it. “Set and tied in” means you stood at it and looked, and you have the photograph; it is never a call that the weld is good, the bolt is tight or the connection holds — that is the special inspector’s, and nothing here is his record. It’s an ask to close out what has to be in the iron before the deck covers it: not an RFI, not a change order, and it doesn’t authorize anybody to do extra work. Double-check it before you send it, because a leave-out nobody walked is a saw cut somebody pays for.

The job — typed once, rides on every copy

Name the set and its revision, and put a name on every row. A weld clip counted against what the electrician told you Tuesday is one he confirms from his truck this afternoon; the same clip described from memory is a phone call after the pour. And whatever somebody told you at the gang box, put it in a row with their name on it.

Each leave-out in the iron

Name the spot once — it becomes a button and it carries. The grid line and the gate carry to the next row too, because you walk one bay at a time. Leave anything blank you didn’t see: an empty field is honest and a guessed one is not.
