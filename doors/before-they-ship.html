Before They Ship

Somebody ordered these openings off a drawing. You are the first person to stand in one with a tape, and everything you find that the drawing doesn’t know — a wall that came out thicker than the throat you’ve got, an opening that swings the other way in the field, a head that’s low, something parked in the swing — is cheap today and a welded frame in three weeks. Walk them once, put a row on each one, and send the floor to whoever has to build them. Your tape, your words, and their opening numbers used as an address, not re-typed as a schedule.

Field readings, not a schedule. Every figure on a row is what your tape read at that opening, and every word is yours. This page supplies no rough-opening dimension, no throat or jamb depth, no clearance or undercut, no tolerance, no anchor or grout requirement, no label value and no hardware set — your approved shop drawings and the people who stamp them own all of that. The architect’s door schedule stays the architect’s: the opening number here is how somebody finds the opening, never a second copy of his document. Double-check it before you send it, because it feeds an order.

The job — typed once, rides on every copy

Name the sheet and its rev. It is the difference between a list the inside man can work from and one installer’s opinion he has to re-walk with you — and if he is holding a later rev than you are, that is its own answer and you want it today.

Each opening

Mark number once — it becomes a button and it carries. The floor and the gate carry to the next row too, because you walk a floor at a time. Leave anything blank you didn’t measure: an empty field is honest and a guessed one is not.
