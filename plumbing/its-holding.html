Plumbing · the caption for the gauge photo

It’s Holding

Five pounds of air, ten foot of head, whatever you put on it. Tap on test and the clock starts. Tap a reading when you walk past. Tap when the inspector looks at it. The photo of the gauge is the evidence — this is the times and the numbers that go under it, and it does not need you to remember when you pumped it up.

No target on this page, and no verdict — on purpose. This tool holds no code table, no required pressure, no required duration and no inspector’s checklist, and it will never say a test passed. It writes down what you read off your gauge and when. What is required on your job comes from the code you are under and the inspector standing there — check it before anybody signs anything.

Mark it — you never type a time

Put it on test before you opened this? Tap the mark anyway, then hit the pencil on that row and put the real time in. It gets labelled time typed in from then on, on the page and in what you send — which is the honest thing, because every other stamp here is the clock’s word and that one is yours.

The record

Tap the pencil to fix a time, fix a reading, add a note, or delete a mark. The somebody looked at it mark opens straight into the note — put the name in while he is still standing there.

What it’s on — fill this when you send it, not at the gauge

What it is and where it is go first, because that is what somebody searches their mail for when this comes back up six months from now — not the date, and never the tool’s name.

Send it

Paste it under the photo of the gauge. Typed text on its own is an assertion; the photo with the clock beside it is a record. Start the next one clears the marks and keeps the job and your name — four sections in a day is normal. Copy first: this lives in this browser on this phone and nowhere else, and a browser you have not opened in a couple of weeks can clear it out.
