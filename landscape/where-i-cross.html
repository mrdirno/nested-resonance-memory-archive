Where I Cross

The drive, the walk, the curb, the patio, the wall footing — every one of them is going to sit on top of a pipe of yours, and every one of them is somebody else’s work that closes on somebody else’s day. The sleeve is the one thing on this job with no drawing, no number and no owner until somebody pays for a core drill. Walk the crossings once, put a row on each one, and send it to the man setting forms — where it crosses in your words, what’s going through it, what you’re putting in, how far it sticks out so he can find it, and the gate it has to beat. After the pour it’s a bore, and it’s your money.

Your crossings, not a drawing. Every row is a place your pipe wants to get through his work, in your words, and the sleeve on each row is what you are putting in off your own approved submittal. This page supplies no sleeve size, no cover depth, no separation from anybody else’s line, no pipe size and no spec — your plans, your submittal and whoever stamps them own all of that. It’s an ask to hold a spot in his work before it closes: not an RFI, not a change order, and it doesn’t authorize anybody to do extra work. Double-check it before you send it, because a crossing you forgot is a saw cut you pay for.

The job — typed once, rides on every copy

Name the sheet and its rev. It is the difference between a list the finisher can work from beside his own pour and one landscaper’s opinion he has to re-walk with you — and if he is holding a later civil than you are, that is its own answer and you want it today.

Each crossing

Name the crossing once — it becomes a button and it carries. The area and the gate carry to the next row too, because you walk a bed at a time. Leave anything blank you didn’t decide: an empty field is honest and a guessed one is not.
